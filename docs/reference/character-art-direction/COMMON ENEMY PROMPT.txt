THE NINEFOLD VEIL — COMMON ENEMY & BOSS DESIGN PROMPT
Version 1.1 — Quarter-View Update

COMMON ENEMY
Design an original enemy for The Ninefold Veil, a Korean mythology-inspired 2D hybrid survival roguelike. The in-game enemy should use a pixel-art-based style and remain readable in a crowded angled top-down quarter-view battlefield. The camera should reveal enough of the ground plane and selected top/side surfaces to communicate depth, while preserving clear combat readability.

DESIGN RULES
- Use clean pixel shapes, a strong silhouette, deliberate color blocks, and a restrained number of readable details.
- Design the enemy's body orientation, feet/contact point, head, and weapon or appendages to read from the quarter-view camera. It should look grounded on the playfield, not like a front-facing portrait pasted onto the ground.
- Keep the enemy's perspective consistent with the player, companions, props, and environment. Avoid flat overhead-only shapes, side-scroller poses, or incompatible camera angles.
- The enemy may be eerie, cute, mischievous, hostile, or uncanny, but must fit the game’s mature dark-fantasy tone.
- Prioritize one defining feature: head shape, mask, horn, weapon, talisman, body outline, or distinctive movement silhouette.
- Use the shared blue-black / charcoal, jade / muted teal, and restrained vermilion palette where appropriate.
- Do not rely on color alone to communicate enemy type or threat.
- Avoid realistic skin textures, noisy gradients, tiny ornaments, excessive glow, excessive particles, and shapes that merge into the background.
- The existing 32–48 logical-pixel range is a preliminary size reference only; select final size based on enemy role and gameplay tests. Canvas bounds may need to exceed the nominal body size for horns, tails, weapons, or silhouettes.
- Create isolated enemy art on a transparent background. Do not include text, UI, environmental scenery, or decorative borders.
- For the initial directional test, use four facing directions (up, down, left, right) where movement and attack behavior require them. Mirroring and diagonal-facing coverage should be decided after testing, not assumed for every enemy.

BOSS DESIGN
Create an original boss with a distinctive silhouette and a meaningful connection to Korean supernatural folklore or the fractured-world setting. Distinguish the boss from common enemies through body structure, posture, head shape, signature weapon or appendage, markings, and attack patterns—not size or glow alone.

The design must support two presentations:
1. A more detailed illustration or pixel-art portrait for the bestiary and boss introduction.
2. A simplified in-game quarter-view representation with readable body shape, attack animation, and clear warning effects.

BOSS COMBAT READABILITY
- Attack telegraphs must be distinguishable from decorative background effects and routine enemy attacks.
- The dangerous area and timing must remain readable against the environment and align with the quarter-view ground plane.
- Make attack direction and hit area clear from the camera angle. A ground-targeted area, forward sweep, line attack, and circular warning should have distinguishable shapes and timing.
- Use shape, movement, and animation as well as color to communicate attack type.
- Pixel-based attack shapes may be enhanced by smooth glow, transparency, and particles, but the core boundary and direction must remain clear.
- Rich effects may emphasize impact, but must not obscure the boss’s body or the actual attack boundary.
- Avoid excessive screen-wide particles, bloom, motion blur, or visual noise that prevents the player from reacting.

HYBRID PRESENTATION
Characters and enemies remain pixel-art-based. Environments may use richer layered 2D depth and controlled lighting, all consistent with an angled top-down quarter-view. Effects may combine pixel-based forms with smooth glow and transparency. This does not authorize a 3D pipeline or engine change.

Do not invent a boss’s narrative role, abilities, or faction unless established in the game design documents.
